Almost everyone in Canadian motorsport calls it Mosport, and almost everyone
picturing it is picturing the big track — the plunge through Turn 2, Moss
Corner, the climb back up to the start–finish straight. That circuit is not
where our days here have been spent.

Ours have been spent on the flat pavement in the infield, with cones.

That sounds like a letdown and it is the opposite. A young rider put straight
onto a full-size circuit spends the day being overwhelmed by scale. The same
rider, on a paved lot with a cone layout, spends the day riding — dozens of
passes at the same corner, each one a chance to fix the thing that went wrong
in the last. The lesson arrives in repetitions, and a lot of pavement and no
grandstand is exactly what produces repetitions.

Our days here have been Super Sonic ones, and the format is the point. Nobody
is timed. There is no grid, no flag, and nothing to win, which removes the one
thing that reliably stops a ten-year-old from learning: the temptation to go
faster than their technique. What is left is vision, body position, and being
smooth enough with the throttle that the bike stays settled underneath them.

Mosport is not the only place this happens. Toni runs Super Sonic days at
Lombardy and at Brechin as well, and the shape of the day is the same wherever
the cones come out — a flat piece of pavement, a layout, and a lot of passes at
the same corner. What changes is the surroundings, not the lesson.

The setting still does its own quiet work. Look past the cones and you can see
the transporters, the paddock buildings, the flags along the top of the hill.
The kids notice. They are riding a mini bike around traffic cones roughly a
kilometre from where Canadian Superbike runs, and the geography of the sport
arranges itself in their heads without anyone having to explain it. This is the
bottom of the same ladder.

As a parent it is also the easiest day of the year to watch. Everything happens
within a hundred metres. You can see the whole exercise, hear the coach, and
watch a correction land — the rider gets it wrong, gets told, comes back around
and gets it right, all inside thirty seconds. On a real circuit your child
disappears for most of every lap and you are left guessing.

Nothing about a day here photographs like racing. There is no start, no pass,
no podium. What it produces shows up somewhere else entirely, weeks later, at
Shannonville or Brechin, when the rider carries more speed into a corner
because their eyes are already at the exit — a habit that was built on a
parking lot in the infield at Mosport.
