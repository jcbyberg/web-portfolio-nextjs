![Title card reading "A trade show booth is nine pieces, not one." — case study for Road Race Canada](/images/whitespace/02-trade-show-is-nine-pieces/title-card.png)

Everyone thinks of the backwall. The backwall is about a third of it.

For Road Race Canada's bike show season I built the full booth: a ten-foot backwall, a five-foot step-and-repeat, three retractable canister banners, hero posters for the T-stands, and the card set for the table.

Toni Sharpless, the client behind Road Race Canada, has more trade show experience than anyone I know. She understands how people move through a busy hall, what catches their attention and what information they need once they stop. That experience set a high bar: every piece had to look polished, communicate quickly and work in the real world—not just on a screen.

Each of those is a different physical object with a different aspect ratio and a different viewing distance, and that changes the design every time. A canister banner is tall and narrow and gets read from four feet by someone already walking past — so it needs one headline you can catch in half a second, with the detail underneath for whoever actually stops. A backwall gets read from across the hall. A hero poster at 22 × 14 is basically a portrait.

The three banners follow that rule exactly. Super Sonic leads with *The First Lap Starts Here* and puts the fundamentals, the locations and the price below it. MiniSBK leads with *Drag a Knee With Us* and carries the full fee structure underneath. FIM MotoMini gets its own. All three put the QR code at eye level, not down by the floor where nobody will crouch for it.

The real design problem isn't any single piece. It's making all nine look like one organization when they are standing in a room together — same red, same type, same logo lockups — while each one is still correct for its own format and its own distance.

Large format is unforgiving in one specific way: you get the fabricator's template, you build to its exact dimensions, and then it goes to print. There is no second try.

Bleed lines and vector graphics are a must for this kind of work. Bleed gives the fabricator the extra printed area needed to trim and finish each piece without leaving an accidental white edge. Safe areas keep logos, type and QR codes away from seams, hems, grommets and hardware. Vector artwork keeps logos, lettering and graphic shapes razor sharp whether they are printed on a small table card or stretched across a ten-foot backwall.

You also have to get comfortable working in Adobe Illustrator. It is where I prepare the large-format layouts, follow the fabricator's dielines, extend artwork through the bleed and package print-ready vector files. A booth may look like one design when it is assembled, but every one of its nine pieces needs its own correctly built production file.
