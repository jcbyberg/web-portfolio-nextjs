Every social platform hands you a form. Name, category, description, phone, address, hours.

That form was built for a business. A shop, with a street number, and a reason for strangers to show up at it.

Then a person fills it in. Or a charity. Or a volunteer setting up a page for a youth program on a Tuesday night. And the form does what it was built to do. It publishes.

We check pages as part of taking on a new client, and this is the thing we find most. Not a bad logo. Not a weak bio. A field somebody filled in honestly years ago that's been publishing something ever since that nobody meant to make public.

## The fields nobody rereads

**The address.** On most platforms this isn't just text. It becomes a clickable map with a pin in it. If the group runs out of somebody's house, that pin is on a house. Nobody types their address in thinking "I am publishing a map to my front door." But the form can't tell a shop from a kitchen table.

**The phone number.** Somebody's personal mobile, entered once during setup because it was the number they had. It's now the organization's public number, forever, ringing on a personal phone at 11pm.

**Reviews.** These are switched on by default for most page types. Fine for a shop. But if the page is one person — a coach, an artist, a young athlete — any stranger can leave a permanent public rating on a named individual, and that person can't take it down.

**The category.** Picking a business category quietly switches on business features: reviews, hours, service areas, quote buttons. A lot of what gets exposed came attached to a dropdown choice made in the first ten minutes.

**The bio.** This one's sneaky, because it's the field people *do* reread. But there's a difference between saying what someone does and saying who and where they are. An age, a school, a neighbourhood, a home town, a description of what they look like — none of that says anything about the work.

## One question sorts it

Ask this about every field on the page:

**Does this say what we do, or does it help a stranger find one specific person?**

That's the whole test. Achievements, services, hours, what you're for, where the public events are — those say what you do. An age, a home address, a school, a regular location, a personal phone — those help someone find a person.

Keep the first kind. Cut the second, and don't argue with yourself about it. Every single piece sounds harmless on its own. They're only a problem together.

That's the part people miss. An age by itself is nothing. A neighbourhood by itself is nothing. A photo by itself is nothing. All three on one page, next to a schedule of where that person will be on Saturday, is something else entirely — and no single edit made it that way.

## When the address is the point

Sometimes you need it published. A food bank people have to get to. A drop-in centre. A community meal.

But not every service wants a pin on a map, and the ones that don't are usually the ones where it matters most. A shelter. A support group. Any program serving people who might be getting looked for. For those, publishing a street address isn't a marketing choice, it's a safety one.

The usual fix is a phone number or an intake email instead, and you give the address to the people who need it. Small bit of friction, big payoff. If that's you and the address is up there because the form asked for one, spend an hour on it this week.

## Do this now

Open your page's edit view. Not the public view — the edit view, where you can see every field at once, including the ones that aren't showing right now. Read them like you've never heard of the organization.

Then do the same on any page you run for someone else. Volunteer pages, program pages, pages for individuals. That's where we find the worst of it, because they got set up fast by someone helpful who's since moved on.

The fix takes about ten minutes. Finding out you needed it is the part that takes years.
