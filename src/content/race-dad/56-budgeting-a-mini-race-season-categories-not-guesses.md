Every new racing family asks what a season costs, and every veteran gives a number that is wrong for them. The right answer is not a number — it is a framework of categories, so you build your own honestly. Here is the framework.

![A wallet with money and coins scattered around it](/images/race-dad/budgeting-a-mini-race-season/budgeting-a-mini-race-season-01.jpg)

## The categories

Entries and memberships: race entries for every round you plan, the series membership, the club licence, the practice-day fees. Count the season you will actually run — the rounds within driving distance — not the season you daydream about in February.

Travel: fuel, accommodation, food on the road. Two-day rounds double it; out-of-province rounds triple-plus it. This category is where budgets quietly die, because it scales with the calendar you signed, not the calendar you budgeted. Our true-cost post walks a season's reality from the family seats; the lesson that stuck with us was that travel, not racing, is the line that grew.

Tires and consumables: tires for the season (count sets by how many rounds you will genuinely replace for, not for speed — for parity with your class's norms), oil and filters, brake pads, premix, chain and sprockets. The consumables are the heartbeat spend — steady, predictable, and non-negotiable once the season starts.

Wear parts and maintenance: the scheduled work from the service calendar — top-end fund on a two-stroke, valve checks on a four-stroke, fork oil, brake fluid, the winter teardown. These are not surprises; they are invoices with dates.

Crash budget: money set aside, physically or mentally, for the season's honest probability — a set of levers, a bodywork panel, a clip-on, maybe worse. Every paddock family learns this line exists; budgeting it in advance is the difference between a bad afternoon and a bad month. If the season ends unspent, it rolls into next year's tire fund. It will not end unspent.

## Building your number

Take your calendar and price each category against it honestly — real fuel distances, real hotel nights, real tire counts for your class. Put the total on paper and then apply the two tests. The calendar test: could you run one fewer round and convert that money into coaching or track time? Sometimes yes, and it is the better spend. The reality test: does the number survive contact with a rainy weekend and a crashed bike in June? A budget with no slack is a plan to quit in July.

## What not to do

Do not adopt someone else's number — another family's calendar, class, and tolerance are not yours, and copied budgets break on contact with your reality. Do not hide the crash budget from yourself; hope is not a line item. And do not let the number become the season's scoreboard. The budget exists so that money stops being the conversation, and the racing can be.

Categories, not guesses: it is the difference between a season you planned and a season that happened to you.
