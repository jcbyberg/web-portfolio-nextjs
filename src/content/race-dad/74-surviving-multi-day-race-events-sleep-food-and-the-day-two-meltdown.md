Day one of a double-header goes fine for almost everyone. Day two is where the plan shows. The kid who slept badly, ate track fries for the second straight meal, and spent four hours between sessions vibrating on a scooter arrives at Sunday's race with nothing left — and loses to riders they beat on Saturday. Multi-day events are won overnight, in the parts of the weekend that feel like downtime.

![A title card reading "Day two is the race." for Race Dad](/images/race-dad/multi-day-event-survival/multi-day-event-survival-01.png)

## The rhythm of a two-day round

The families who survive double-headers run a schedule, not a vibe. Everything that can be done Saturday night gets done Saturday night: the bike checked and fuelled, the gear laid out, the cooler restocked, the pit organized so Sunday morning is walking, not thinking. A rough plan for each day — when to arrive, when the rider eats, when they rest, when the bike goes on the stand — turns a weekend from a marathon of reacting into a series of small events. Kids are spectacular at routines and terrible at chaos; give them the routine.

## Food that is not track fries

The paddock food truck is a treat, not a food group. A racer's weekend runs on the cooler you brought: real breakfast, lunch that is not a nap in sandwich form, snacks with substance, and a water bottle that gets refilled so often the kid is annoyed at you. The pattern to avoid is the sugar-and-fries cycle — high at two, gone by three, cranky by four, and Sunday's race starts at ten. You do not need a nutrition program. You need to not outsource a growing athlete's fuel to the nearest fryer.

## Sleep, in heat and in cold

Sleep is the whole ballgame on multi-day events, and it is where the planning happens months earlier. Hot weekend: shade over the tent by afternoon, a fan or battery station if you have one, lighter sleeping bags than May looks like it needs. Cold weekend: real sleeping bags, layers, toques — kids who sleep cold wake up wrecked and ride like it. If the venue allows camping, set up away from the generator row and the late-night paddock social; if not, a nearby room booked before the season beats a midnight highway search. Whatever the arrangement, protect the wind-down: screens off, lights low, the same bedtime both nights.

## Downtime management

A bored, tired kid at hour six of a paddock day is a slow rider on Sunday morning. The gear that earns its place by day three: chairs, shade, a cooler that is actually stocked, something for the rider to do that is not a screen in full sun — cards, a ball, the other kids. Schedule a real rest block after lunch, in the shade, flat if possible. And watch the parents' own fuel: the adult who has stood in the sun for two days making bad decisions on Sunday morning is also a race factor. Day two is the race. Everything you do on day one is either loading the odds for it or against it.
