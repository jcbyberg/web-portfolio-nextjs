No paddock argument is more durable than premix ratio — everyone's cousin runs 32:1 and the manual says something boring, and the new family just wants a number. Here is how to get it right without the theology.

![A title card reading "Measure twice, mix once." for Race Dad](/images/race-dad/two-stroke-mix-ratios/two-stroke-mix-ratios-01.png)

## What the ratio means

The ratio is fuel-to-oil by volume: 50:1 means fifty parts fuel to one part oil. The oil is not a diet — it is the engine's bottom-end lubrication, mixed into the fuel because a two-stroke has no separate oil supply. Too little oil and the crank and cylinder run metal-on-metal — that is the seized engine. Too much oil and you richen the mixture, foul plugs, smoke like a mosquito fogger, and deposit carbon — that is the slow, sad version of the same neglect. The window is wide enough for life and narrow enough to respect.

## What the manual says versus the paddock

The rule that ends the argument: the manufacturer's ratio for your bike is correct, and the cast of characters at the track with other numbers are describing other bikes, other oils, other eras. Modern quality two-stroke oil at the manual's ratio is the engineering answer — the people who built the engine did the metallurgy. If you switch oil brands or types (castor, synthetic, mineral blends carry different expectations), follow that oil's documentation for your application. The paddock's 32:1-in-everything folklore descends from older oils and richer jetting eras; it is not wrong about history, and it is not your manual.

## Measuring reliably

The disasters are all measurement disasters. Use a marked ratio cup or a graduated jug — not the "glug, glug, that looks right" method, not a water bottle with a line on it. Mix in a dedicated container, add the oil to the fuel (or per your oil's directions), and shake it — actually agitate it, because oil settles and a stratified jug is straight fuel at the top. Mix fresh: premix ages, separates, and loses its enthusiasm; mix what the weekend needs, store it labeled, and date the jug.

## The one-jug-one-bike system

The 7am disaster is always the same story: two bikes, two ratios, one family, one unmarked jug. The system is one jug per bike, labeled with painter's tape and marker — the bike's name, the ratio, the oil — and the jugs never meet. Different ratio bikes in one family is normal; mixing them up is a top-end lottery. Color-coded jugs, colored tape, whatever makes the wrong jug visually loud at dawn.

Measure twice, mix once. The engine's life is decided in that twenty seconds at the fuel station, not on the track.
