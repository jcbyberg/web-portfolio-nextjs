Every paddock has the story. The bike that fell over at a red light twenty minutes from home, the strap that let go on the highway ramp, the machine that arrived at the track already crashed. Tying down a bike is a five-minute skill that protects every dollar of the season, and almost everybody does it slightly wrong.

![A title card reading "Strap it like you love it." for Race Dad](/images/race-dad/tying-down-bikes-securely/tying-down-bikes-securely-01.png)

## The technique

Soft loops at proper anchor points, always. A strap's hook directly on a handlebar or footpeg is how bars get scratched, cables get pinched, and hooks slip off at speed — soft loops around a structural point, hooks to the loops. On minis, the good anchors are the triple clamp area below the bars, the frame spars, or purpose-made tie-down points; never plastic bodywork, never exhausts, never anything that moves with the suspension.

Compression: enough forks squeeze to hold the bike — roughly a third of travel is the common guidance — but not bottomed-out hard. The suspension is a spring holding the straps tight; over-compress it for a long drive and you are asking the fork seals to spend the trip under max load. Angle matters: straps run forward and outward from the bars at roughly forty-five degrees make a stable triangle; four points (two front, two rear) is the standard for highway travel, and with minis the rear pair is cheap insurance the first time you meet a rough road at speed.

The kill shot most people never think about: bike position on its wheels, in gear or strapped so it cannot roll, with the load balanced and nothing leaning. A bike strapped to the side of a trailer wall "because it fit better" is a sail with a strap count.

## What breaks when you get it wrong

The famous failure is the strap that stretches or the anchor that lets go, and one loose strap un-weights the whole system — the bike walks sideways, then everything lets go at once. The quieter failures cost more over a season: a hook through a radiator, a strap chewed by a chain, bars bent by a compression-and-vibration combo nobody noticed at the first stop. Which is the actual core skill: checking at every stop. Straps settle, compress relaxes, temperature changes tension. First stop twenty minutes out, then every fuel or food stop — a ten-second walk-around, every strap, every time. The families who never have the story are the ones for whom the walk-around is boring reflex.

## The straps themselves

Cam-buckle straps are fine for short hauls; ratchets for highway and long trips, with the caveat that ratchets make over-compression easy — measure your squeeze by fork travel, not by feel-strong. Replace any strap with frayed stitching, a sticky buckle, or a hook that has been bent and re-bent; they are consumables, not heirlooms. And if you are still using the straps that came free with something in 2019, this is the sign.

The season starts in the driveway — that is not a metaphor. Load height, tie-down points, and the rest of the trailer question is its own post; this one is just the five minutes that makes sure the bike arrives the way it left. Strap it like you love it, check it every stop, and may the story in your paddock stay someone else's.
