Ask a girl on the mini grid what would keep her racing, and the answer is almost never about her own speed — it is about whether the paddock is a place she gets to keep being fast in. Equipment that fits. People who are glad she is there. Somewhere up the ladder, other women going fast where she can see them. None of that is radical. All of it is doable by ordinary families and ordinary clubs, starting now.

![A title card reading "Fast has no gender." for Race Dad](/images/race-dad/girls-on-the-mini-grid/girls-on-the-mini-grid-01.png)

## Equipment that fits

The first barrier is material: gear cut for nobody in the class. Kids' leathers sized down from adult patterns put armour in the wrong places on any small rider, and the fit problems compound for girls — chest, hips, and proportions the size charts did not have in mind. This is exactly the case for made-to-measure gear that our custom kids' suits post makes: armour only protects the joints it is actually sitting on, and a rider whose suit fits is a rider who can move on the bike instead of wearing it. If a maker will measure your daughter properly, that is the maker worth calling.

## Paddocks that welcome

The second barrier is atmosphere, and it is built from small things. Names used correctly. The coaching delivered the same way it is delivered to the boys — same drills, same tone, same expectations, because talking down to a fast girl is how you lose a fast girl. Parents hold most of the cards here: who your kid's heroes are allowed to be, whether the pit-lane chat treats her results as racing results rather than a novelty. A club signals its paddock in a hundred small ways, and families choosing where to race can read them in one visit.

## Visibility up the ladder

The third is what she can see from the mini grid. There are fast women racing at national and world level — road racing's front rows are not the boys-only picture some families still have in their heads, and the girls on mini grids who follow those riders know the ladder has room on it. What the sport needs is for that visibility to reach the paddock level: clubs listing their female racers the same way they list any racer, media covering them as racers, and parents pointing their daughters at the whole grid of people who look like their futures.

## What parents and organizers can actually do

Parents: gear that fits, coaching that expects, and zero tolerance for the paddock characters who treat the grid as a boys' club — most of them respond to being told, once, plainly. Organizers: the same registration, the same classes, the same championship points, and photos in the results gallery that show the whole grid. None of this requires a program or a budget; it requires doing ordinary things consistently. I am not going to quote participation numbers, because the ones that matter are the ones in your own paddock: count your grid this season, and if a fast girl leaves the sport because the paddock was easier to leave than to belong to, that is the statistic that counts. Fast has no gender. The sport gets to decide whether the paddock does.
