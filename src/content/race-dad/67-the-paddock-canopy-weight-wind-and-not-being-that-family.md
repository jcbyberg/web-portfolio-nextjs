Every track has a flying-canopy story. Ours involves a ten-by-twenty pop-up, an unexpected Saturday gust, a full paddock watching it cartwheel toward a row of parked minis, and a father sprinting in crocs. The canopy was fine. Nobody was hurt. The family's weekend, and reputation, took the hit. Canopy craft is a racing skill.

![A title card reading "Anchor like the wind is coming." for Race Dad](/images/race-dad/paddock-canopy-setup/paddock-canopy-setup-01.png)

## Weights, not pegs

Most minimoto venues are asphalt paddocks, and pegs do not work on asphalt — a canopy staked to nothing is a kite with legs. The rule that ends the argument: weight on every leg, low, so the canopy pushes its anchors into the ground instead of levering them over. Proper canopy weights, sandbags, water jugs — enough that you cannot rock the frame with two hands. The test is not whether it stands on a calm morning; it is whether it stands when the sky changes at two in the afternoon, because it will.

## Sidewalls and the wind

Sidewalls turn a canopy into a sail. In a breeze they are shade and privacy; when the wind arrives they are the thing that lifts the whole structure. The habit that works: sidewalls out while you need them, and the moment the forecast or the flags say wind, the windward wall comes down first, then the rest. If the gusts are serious, drop the legs and let the canopy ride low — a flattened canopy is a conversation, an airborne one is an incident.

## Where to set up

Placement is etiquette as much as tactics. Leave the fire lane clear. Face your open side away from the prevailing wind. Do not back your trailer into your neighbour's pit view without asking, and do not strap your canopy to their structure without asking either — your canopy's failure mode should not involve their bike. Near pit lane is convenient and loud; the far paddock is quiet and a walk. Families with young riders usually win by being close to the pit entrance and the washrooms, because a hundred saved steps times forty trips is a saved afternoon.

## Storm protocol

Decide before the weather arrives. Watch the sky, not the schedule: a black wall to the west means gear off the tables, loose items in the bins, bikes on stands under the canopy legs lowered, and if it looks genuinely ugly, the canopy comes down completely and everything loose goes in the trailer. The twenty minutes you lose re-setting up are nothing. Comfort is speed in this sport — a rider with shade, a chair, and a calm corner of the paddock conserves the energy they will spend on track. Anchor like the wind is coming, because it is coming. The only question is whether your family is the story or the audience.
